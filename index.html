TL;DR A visual policy trained on one robot fails when a different arm appears in the camera. UniLatent renders paired images of both robots at the same gripper pose in simulation and trains the source encoder to match a frozen target encoder. A policy trained on encoded source demonstrations then runs on the target robot through that frozen encoder. No image is translated or generated at any point.

Abstract

Robot datasets are costly to collect and are usually tied to a specific embodiment, which makes it hard to reuse demonstrations across robots that differ in geometry, appearance and kinematics. We introduce UniLatent, a cross-embodiment transfer framework that avoids pixel-level image translation. UniLatent renders paired source–target robot observations in simulation and aligns a source visual encoder to a fixed target encoder, producing a shared latent space for policy learning. A policy trained on encoded source demonstrations can then be deployed on target observations through the target encoder. Across three simulation tasks and two target embodiments, UniLatent reaches up to 93% zero-shot transfer success and improves mean target success over the strongest pixel-translation baseline by more than 30 percentage points. In real-world xArm-to-Franka few-shot transfer, UniLatent completes 27 of 30 target rollouts, consistently outperforming target-only training and pixel-space translation pretraining.

The problem

A policy trained on Franka does not transfer to Sawyer.

In third-person views the robot fills much of the image. Changing the arm alters appearance, self-occlusion and what the camera can see of the task, all at once. Both policies below, shown on Stack Cup into Bowl, reach 80–89% on the source Franka.

Prior work closes this gap by editing the source frames with masking, inpainting or diffusion-based robot generation. The policy then consumes edited pixels, so distorted grippers and inconsistent contact regions propagate straight into closed-loop control.

The idea

Align representations, not pixels.

Render a source and a target robot holding the same tool-center-point pose, under the same camera and the same randomized scene. These pairs teach a source encoder to map source images into the feature space of a frozen target encoder. The renderer only has to establish correspondence. It never has to look photorealistic, because the policy never sees a rendered target image.

Train the policy. Source demonstrations are encoded with the frozen ES. A conditional flow-matching policy learns to predict end-effector action chunks (H = 8) from a short latent history (k = 3).

Deploy on the target. The target robot's own camera images go through the same frozen ET, and the same policy acts on them in closed loop. Nothing is translated or generated at test time.

Why freeze the target encoder

The features seen at deployment come from exactly the model that defined the alignment objective. Training only the source side asks each source image for the representation of its matched target image, instead of pushing both robots into a shared space that neither occupies at test time.

Why latent transfer helps

Translated frames look right and act wrong.

A translated frame can keep the global layout while getting the wrist, the gripper opening or the contact region subtly wrong, and a closed-loop policy amplifies exactly those errors. UniLatent uses the same paired renderings to align features, and never trains the policy on synthesized pixels.

What we compare. Prior cross-embodiment methods make a source-robot frame look like the target robot, then train a policy on those edited pixels. The figure takes five source frames (top row) and shows what three translation strategies turn them into, next to the true target rendering (bottom row). A good translation would match the bottom row exactly.

RoVi-Aug

Diffusion cross-painting (SDXL + ControlNet) repaints the robot as the target.

Under hard poses it distorts links and joint structure. It is also slow, about 10 s per frame, or roughly 17 minutes per 10 s demonstration.

Shadow*

Segmentation masking replaces the robot with a flat silhouette.

The mask hides the gripper and the contact region, which are exactly the cues manipulation depends on.

RoVi-Aug*

Decodes the aligned source features into target-looking images, trained on the same paired renderings as UniLatent.

The closest match to the target, yet still a little off at the wrist and gripper. In simulation it reaches 44.5% mean target success against 76% for UniLatent.

RoVi-Aug* in closed loop

RoVi-Aug* policy rollouts on both target robots, zero-shot from Franka demonstrations. Success rates are over 100 rollouts (Table 1).

Takeaway

RoVi-Aug* and UniLatent learn from the same paired renderings, so the gap between them measures pixel-space against latent-space transfer: 44.5% against 76% mean target success in simulation. On the real robot, the comparison is with the original diffusion-based RoVi-Aug used for pretraining: 23/30 against 27/30 for UniLatent.

Limitations

What this does not cover

UniLatent targets single-arm robots with parallel-jaw grippers and a shared end-effector action interface. Dexterous hands, mobile manipulators and tasks that need tool-specific contact would require additional retargeting and contact modeling. The method assumes the target robot can complete source tasks within a comparable workspace. The real-world evaluation uses a single physical setup, so broader background and object variation remain future work.
